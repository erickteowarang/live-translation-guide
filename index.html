More quirks · 難しい理由

The rest of the obstacle course.

Each of these makes the interpreter hold ambiguity in their head while you keep talking.

01

Negation hides at the end否定は文末

You just saw it: できます / できません, 行く / 行かない. Whether a sentence is yes or no is decided by its final syllables.

02

Politeness levels敬語

Honorific, humble, and plain registers encode who ranks where. English has no equivalent, so the nuance is flattened away or spelled out with extra words and time.

03

"Hai" isn't "yes"相槌

はい・ええ・なるほど are backchannels meaning "I'm listening," not "I agree." Read as agreement, they manufacture consensus no one gave.

04

Counters change numbers助数詞

"One" is 一本・一枚・一匹・一つ depending on what you're counting. The interpreter needs the noun before they can even say the number.

05

Homophones everywhere同音異義語

はし is bridge, chopsticks, or edge (橋・箸・端). Only context — sometimes not yet spoken — tells them apart.

06

Topic vs. subject「は」と「が」

The particles は and が shift emphasis and what's "given" vs. "new" in ways English marks with word order and stress instead.

Your primary tool · Google Meet

Why this matters even more on Google Meet.

The habits above help with any interpreter, human or machine. But Google Meet's translation works in a specific way — and one detail changes how you should use it.

JP ⇄ EN

You'll read captions, not hear a voice.

Meet's spoken translation — the synthetic voice — doesn't cover Japanese. Japanese⇄English runs as translated text captions: each person sets their own caption language and reads along. So the goal is clean text appearing on screen, fast enough to follow — which is exactly what these tips protect.

Audio in

It transcribes first, then translates.

Meet turns speech into text before translating it. A weak mic, room noise, or two people at once corrupts the transcript first — and the translation faithfully carries the error downstream. Good audio and one speaker at a time matter more here than anywhere.

Live rewrite

The caption rewrites itself as you talk.

Meet shows a running guess and revises earlier words as more speech arrives. Because Japanese saves the verb for last, a caption can read as "yes" and then flip to "no" — the できます → できません flip from the demo, live on screen. Finish the sentence, then pause and let the text settle.

Latency

There's a few-second lag — wait for it.

The caption trails the speaker by a beat, and Meet shows an indicator while it catches up. Don't reply the instant someone stops — the line you're answering may not have finished translating.

For everyone in the room · 全員へ

Speak so the interpreter can keep up.

These habits help whichever direction you're speaking — and they help human interpreters and live AI captioning alike.

01

One idea per sentence

Short sentences give the interpreter a place to catch up. Long run-ons force them to buffer everything at once.

02

Pause at full stops

Finish a thought, then leave a beat of silence. That gap is where the rendering actually happens.

03

One speaker at a time

Overlapping voices can't both be interpreted. Wait for the rendering to finish before you reply.

04

Share materials first

Agenda, slides, names, and key terms in advance let the interpreter prepare vocabulary instead of improvising it.

05

Spell out names & numbers

Say acronyms in full the first time; repeat critical figures and dates slowly. Numbers have no context to recover them from.

06

Drop idioms & jokes

"Circle back," "ballpark," puns, and sports metaphors don't cross over. Say the plain meaning instead.

Depends on your language · 話す言語別

Then, a few things just for your side.

The hardest gaps run in one direction. Pick the track for the language you speak.

If you speak Japanese

JP → EN

日本語で話す人へ — help your ideas survive the trip into English.

  • →Say the subject out loud. Name who or what you mean (誰が・何が), even when it feels obvious. English can't leave it blank.
  • →Conclusion first. 結論から — state the result, then the reasons, so no one waits for the verb.
  • →Finish your sentences. Avoid trailing off with 「…が。」「…けど。」 The ending carries the meaning.
  • →Ease off stacked keigo. Clear, plainly polite speech renders faster than layered honorifics.
  • →Break up long modifiers. Split a big 「〜という〜」 clause into two short sentences.

If you speak English

EN → JP

英語で話す人へ — most English speakers simply go too fast.

  • →Slow down and chunk. Native pace is faster than anyone can render live. Deliver in short, complete pieces.
  • →Cut the idioms. "Low-hanging fruit," "let's table this," "ballpark" — say the literal thing you mean.
  • →One clause at a time. Avoid long chains of subordinate clauses ("which, given that…, means…").
  • →Pause after questions. Give room for the question to be rendered and the answer to come back before you fill the silence.
  • →Don't answer early. Wait for the full interpretation to finish — the last few words may change the meaning.
